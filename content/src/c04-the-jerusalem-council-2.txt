Acts relates that Barnabas wanted to bring John, called Mark, with them. Paul disagreed, because Mark had left them in Pamphylia.[[fn:Acts 15:37–39.]] The quarrel drove them to part and go their separate ways.[[fn:Acts 15:35–41.]] Acts records:

{{kjv:Acts 15:39-40}}

The quarrel between Barnabas and Paul reflects a basic difference in their approach to the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. Barnabas held to the original message of <em>tawḥīd</em> and the law, as preserved by the <em>Urgemeinde</em>, the original community of the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam in Islamicjerusalem, led by James and supported by Peter. Paul leaned ever further toward adapting the teaching to pagan culture to attract Gentile followers. That change was a theological deviation, and it ended in a great split in the body of the early church.

Peter, regarded as the chief leader of the disciples, stressed obedience to the Jewish law. Acts records his preaching:

{{kjv:Acts 2:40}}

That approach differs from Paul's looser attitude to the obligations of Jewish law.[[fn:Galatians 2:16.]]

## Open conflict with Peter

At the same time Paul was in open dispute with Cephas (Peter), as his letter to the Galatians records:

{{kjv:Galatians 2:11-14}}

The passage reflects the basic tension between Paul's teaching, which leaned toward the Gentiles, and the teaching of the disciples, who held to the law of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, including obedience to the Torah. The disciples rejected Paul's theological adaptation because it undermined the foundation of <em>tawḥīd</em> and the authenticity of the message of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

Paul tells the scene as a rebuke he delivered, and he leaves unsaid who carried the day. By his own account the other Jewish believers followed Peter, and even Barnabas "was carried away with their dissimulation."[[fn:Galatians 2:13.]] Michael Goulder draws the plain conclusion: the church at Antioch sided with Peter against Paul, and "the Peter party, the Petrines, had won the round."[[fn:Michael Goulder, <em>St. Paul versus St. Peter: A Tale of Two Missions</em> (Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 1995), p. 3. Goulder presents the Two Missions thesis as his own reading, "not an amalgam of standard opinions," and as a revival of the proposal Ferdinand Baur made in 1831 (p. 194).]] The first recorded dispute between Paul and the disciples, reported by Paul himself, ended in his defeat.

The letters and Acts also disagree on where Paul carried his message. Paul calls himself the apostle to the Gentiles and records a division of labour agreed with the pillars in Jerusalem: he and Barnabas to the Gentiles, they to the circumcised.[[fn:Galatians 1:16; 2:7–9.]] Acts sends him to the synagogue first in city after city, and turns him to the Gentiles only when the Jews refuse him.[[fn:Acts 13:5, 14, 46; 14:1; 17:1–4, 10, 17; 18:4–6; 19:8–9.]] The church at Thessalonica shows which account is history. Paul's letter to it addresses converts who "turned to God from idols," a congregation of former pagans in which no Jewish believer appears; Acts gives the same church Jewish converts, won in three sabbaths of argument in the synagogue.[[fn:1 Thessalonians 1:9; Acts 17:1–4.]] Martin's verdict on the account in Acts was blunt: "It is fiction."[[fn:Dale B. Martin, notes on "Luke" and Paul, 2019, "Paul’s Missionary Practices," published on this site with his permission: [paulus_link slug="dale-b-martin-luke-versus-paul" anchor="m-missionary" /].]]

At first Paul's call found no welcome among Gentiles, because of their hatred of the Jews. For that reason Paul began to distance himself from Barnabas and set his mind on spreading the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam to the Gentiles on his own terms.
