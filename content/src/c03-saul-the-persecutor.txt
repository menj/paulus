## Hatred without cause

The violence Saul showed in hunting the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam was driven by more than religious duty or obedience to the highest religious authority. It reflects a deep ideological hatred. In Jewish moral tradition, hatred of this kind is called <em>sinat chinam</em> (שִׂנְאַת חִנָּם, "hatred without cause"), hatred with no legitimate reason or real threat behind it. The term is found in the Psalms[[fn:cf. Psalms 35:19, 69:4.]] and in the Babylonian Talmud,[[fn:Yoma 9b.]] where it is named the chief cause of the fall of the Second Temple.[[fn:See Jonathan Sacks, <em>Not in God's Name: Confronting Religious Violence</em> (New York: Schocken Books, 2017), p. 218; and Rabbi Doron Perez, "Sinat Chinam: From Dispute to Demonization," <em>HaMizrachi</em>, Tisha B'Av 2020, p. 3.]]

Paul's conduct in the early part of his life fits that character: full of anger, hating without ground, ready to use violence to wipe out those who differed. His hostility to the <em>ḥawāriyyūn</em> was a hatred without cause, rooted in inner unease and in the threat their message posed to his own religious identity.

Saul then set out for Damascus to hunt the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. On that road, he claimed, he "met" Jesus Christ through an apparition from the sky. Suddenly he declared that he had repented of his crimes against the supporters of the mission of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, and that he was the most faithful follower of Jesus Christ.
