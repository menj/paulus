If Christian theology was shaped for a popular audience, how did Paul's writings come to dominate the New Testament over the gospels that record the direct teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him)? Why are most of the books of the New Testament letters of Paul and so few the records of the disciples?

## The language of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam

Any assessment of Paul's dominance in the New Testament has to reckon with the language and culture of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam himself. Historical and linguistic study shows that the main language he used in his life and preaching was Aramaic, the language of Palestine at the time. The Gospel of Mark preserves several of his phrases in Aramaic even though the whole text is in Greek: "Talitha cumi,"[[fn:Mark 5:41, טַלִיתָא קוּמִי (<em>ṭalītā qūmī</em>): "Damsel, arise."]] "Ephphatha"[[fn:Mark 7:34, אֶפְתַּח (<em>ʾeptaḥ</em>): "Be opened."]] and "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?"[[fn:Mark 15:34, אֵלִי אֵלִי לָמָה עֲזַבְתָּנִי (<em>ʾēlī ʾēlī lāmā ʿazavtānī</em>): "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"]]

That shows the original Injīl was very probably delivered in Aramaic, and what we have inherited in Greek is a rendering or a secondary narrative. Some scholars hold that the Gospel of Matthew once existed in Aramaic before it was lost to time. The dominance of Greek in the New Testament text should therefore be read as a reframing of the original revelation in a foreign cultural and linguistic setting.[[fn:See Bruce M. Metzger, <em>The Early Versions of the New Testament: Their Origin, Transmission, and Limitations</em> (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1977), pp. 103–105.]]

## The gospels that were set aside

{{fig:nag-hammadi|Nag Hammadi Codex II, fourth century, Coptic Museum, Cairo, with the Gospel of Thomas and other texts the churches that followed Paul did not keep.}}

In the time of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam he had many followers who wrote down his sermons and teachings after his departure. Foremost among them were the twelve apostles named in the New Testament. From the records of these followers come the writings collected under the name "gospel," each named for its writer: the Gospel of Marcion, the Gospel of the Egyptians, the Gospel of Eve, the Gospel of Judas, the Gospel of Nicodemus, the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Matthias, the Gospel of the Twelve, the Gospel of Truth, the Gospel of Mary, the Gospel of Andrew, the Gospel of Philip, and others.[[fn:Most of these texts were found in the Nag Hammadi collection of manuscripts in Egypt in 1945.]]

These gospels are said to contain the teaching of Satan and to contradict the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. In truth they are the gospels closest to the original Injīl, never yet infiltrated by the teachings of Paul.

The gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke and John are the ones that teach the false doctrines. Luke, who is said to have written the Gospel of Luke, was Paul's personal physician.[[fn:Colossians 4:14, in a letter written in Paul's name; Philemon 1:24, in one of his own, names Luke among his fellow workers.]] In writing his "gospel" he brought in Paul's teachings of his own accord. The proof is in Acts, also written by the author of Luke, where the inclination to exalt Paul is unmistakable.[[fn:Acts 13:9; 16:10–17; 20:5–21:18.]] Luke all but makes a cult of Paul in his writing.

## Paul wrote first

In the chronology of composition, Paul's letters are among the earliest written documents in the New Testament. Letters such as 1 Thessalonians and Galatians are believed to have been written in the 50s CE, a decade or two after the departure of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. The Synoptic Gospels (Mark, Matthew and Luke) were written later, between about 65 and 90 CE, and the Gospel of John around 95 to 100 CE. Historically, then, Paul's teaching came before the written gospels and very probably shaped the theology they contain.[[fn:See Bruce M. Metzger, <em>The New Testament: Its Background, Growth, and Content</em> (Nashville: Abingdon Press, 2003), pp. 98–99.]]

So it is no surprise that of the 27 books of the New Testament, 14 are said to be Paul's.[[fn:The texts Paul wrote are better called "epistles," since they were communications addressed to particular congregations in their religious context.]] Only seven of them are widely accepted by scholars as authentic writings of Paul.[[fn:Romans, 1 Corinthians, 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon. See Bart D. Ehrman, <em>Forged: Writing in the Name of God: Why the Bible's Authors Are Not Who We Think They Are</em> (HarperOne, 2011); Raymond E. Brown, <em>An Introduction to the New Testament</em> (Anchor Bible Reference Library, 1997); E. P. Sanders, <em>Paul: The Apostle's Life, Letters, and Thought</em> (Fortress Press, 2015).]] The other letters in the New Testament are the work of Paul's followers and supporters.[[fn:The other letters meant are Hebrews, 2 Peter, 2 John, 3 John, James, Jude and Revelation (ibid.).]]

## Two Pauls: the man and the canon

The count divides the case in two. Six letters carry Paul's name and are doubted or rejected as his: 2 Thessalonians, Colossians and Ephesians, and the three Pastoral letters to Timothy and Titus, which most scholars assign to his school after his death. The letter to the Hebrews names no author at all, and the church credited it to him only later.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, "Paul's Disciples," Lecture 17, RLST 152: Introduction to New Testament History and Literature, Open Yale Courses, Yale University, Spring 2009, https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152; Dale B. Martin, "The 'Household' Paul: The Pastorals," Lecture 19, RLST 152: Introduction to New Testament History and Literature, Open Yale Courses, Yale University, Spring 2009, https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152.]]

This site keeps the two apart. Where a charge concerns Paul himself, what he taught and did, it rests on the seven letters that scholars accept as his, with Acts as a later and weaker witness. Where a charge concerns the religion that bears his name, all thirteen letters count, because the church received every one of them as Paul's, read them as scripture and built its doctrine on them. A letter written in his name is marked as such wherever it is quoted.

The early church itself shows why the line matters. In the second century two opposed movements each claimed Paul: the Pastoral letters made him the patron of the ordered household, marriage and obedient wives, and the Acts of Paul and Thecla made him the preacher of virginity who drew women out of their homes. Each wrote its Paul in his name.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, "The 'Anti-household' Paul: Thecla," Lecture 20, RLST 152: Introduction to New Testament History and Literature, Open Yale Courses, Yale University, Spring 2009, https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152.]] The Paul of the canon is the Paul the church chose, and the religion of Paul is built on that choice.

## Letters that contradict themselves

The dominance reaches beyond the number of texts or the order of writing into the content and the doctrine. Paul's letters show internal contradictions that cannot be ignored, between the doctrine of one letter and another, and sometimes within the same letter. If a man claims to deliver the revelation of God, the consistency of his teaching is the <em>sine qua non</em> of the claim.

On justification, Paul writes to the Romans that the doers of the law shall be justified, and a chapter later that by the deeds of the law no flesh shall be justified. Both verses speak of the law itself, so the usual defence, that Paul sets the works of the law apart from good deeds in general, has nothing to hold on to here.

That creates a theological tension between faith and works, and leaves open whether the law still applies. On another matter he states in 1 Corinthians 6:11 that Christians have been sanctified and justified, but in Romans 7:19–24 he admits that he goes on struggling with a sin he cannot leave.

{{kjv:Romans 7:19-24}}

On the law, Paul insists that believers stand free of it[[fn:Romans 6:14.]] and that Christ is the end of the law.[[fn:Romans 10:4.]] Yet in Romans 3:31 and 7:12 he says the law is good and must be upheld.

{{kjv:Romans 3:31}}

{{kjv:Romans 7:12}}

The contradiction shows a basic uncertainty about the place of law in religious life.

The sharpest contradictions concern women. In 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 he states plainly that women must keep silent in the assembly and may say nothing. In 1 Corinthians 11:5 he permits women to prophesy and pray openly, provided they cover their heads. That is a basic inconsistency about the public role of women in the Christian assembly.

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 14:34-35}}

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 11:5}}

In Galatians 3:28 Paul declares that there is "neither male nor female" in Christ, as if endorsing absolute equality. To the Corinthians he writes that "the head of the woman is the man," and requires her to cover her head when she prays.[[fn:1 Corinthians 11:3–10.]] The letters written in his name carry the rule further: in Ephesians 5:22–24 wives are ordered to submit to their husbands because the husband is the "head" of the wife. The principle of Galatians does not survive either text.

More confusing still, Paul praises women such as Phoebe as a διάκονος (<em>diákonos</em>, minister of the church)[[fn:Romans 16:1–2.]] and calls Junia "of note among the apostles,"[[fn:Romans 16:7.]] but the first letter to Timothy, written in his name, forbids women to teach or hold authority over men.[[fn:1 Timothy 2:12.]] Once again, actual practice conflicts with the doctrinal rule he wants to enforce.

These contradictions are more than editorial slips or matters of style. What we have inherited as "holy epistles" is, read <em>in toto</em>, a set of mutually contradictory letters from the pen of a man tossed between personal inspiration and the desire to master the message of <em>tawḥīd</em> (توحيد, "the oneness of God") brought by the prophets.
