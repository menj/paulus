The teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) on <em>tawḥīd</em> (توحيد, "the oneness of God") was a complete rejection of the existence of many gods. A teaching of that kind was hard for the Romans to accept, above all among the Gentiles.[[fn:Also called <em>gôyîm</em> (גּוֹיִם), from the Latin <em>gentilis</em>, "belonging to a nation or people"; cf. Douglas Harper, "Gentile," <em>Online Etymology Dictionary</em>, https://bit.ly/4muqlVp (accessed 19 December 2024).]] For a man as sincere and steadfast as Barnabas, living the way of life ʿĪsā ibn Maryam taught among the Romans, without compromise or any attempt to adapt to their culture, must have been a heavy responsibility.

Barnabas held firmly to the belief that ʿĪsā ibn Maryam knew his Creator did not want the laws of the Torah reduced or altered in the smallest degree.[[fn:cf. Matthew 5:18.]] So he stayed loyal to the teaching he had received directly from him. Paul did not agree.

## Tension over the law

After more than two years of preaching among the Gentiles, Paul and Barnabas returned to Antioch in Syria and reported to the congregation on what they claimed as the work of God.[[fn:Acts 14:26–27.]] The rapid growth among uncircumcised Gentiles began to create a new tension, one that had flared before but was now sharpening.[[fn:The question first arose after the conversion of Cornelius, a Gentile, as recorded in Acts 10:1–48 and 11:1–18.]] Many Jews held that Gentiles had to be circumcised and submit to the law of the Torah before they could be fully accepted into the covenant people. The tension led to the need for a great council in Islamicjerusalem to resolve the crisis that was beginning to threaten the unity of the early church.[[fn:Acts 15:1–6.]]

The central question at the Jerusalem Council was whether Gentiles who embraced the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam had to be circumcised and keep the law of the Torah.[[fn:Acts 15:1–2.]] Paul insisted that the requirement was unnecessary, while disciples such as Peter and Barnabas defended the basic requirement that the law be kept.

Peter spoke at the council to recall that God had shown his acceptance of Gentiles through the Cornelius episode. He did not, however, cancel the principle of the law as a whole.[[fn:Acts 15:7–11.]] James then announced a compromise: Gentiles were released from circumcision and remained bound to avoid idolatry, blood, the meat of strangled animals and fornication.[[fn:Acts 15:19–20.]] That shows the original teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, founded on the law, was kept as the basis, even as it was adapted for preaching to other peoples.

{{kjv:Acts 15:19-20}}

Paul, however, interpreted the decision freely and developed the idea that the law of the Torah had ceased to bind anyone, the Jews included. The clash between Paul and the disciples therefore arose from a deep difference of principle, and from more than a difference of approach.

## Playing the Jew in the Temple

One event that exposes Paul's plain hypocrisy took place when he returned to Islamicjerusalem after his missions in Gentile territory. As Acts records,[[fn:Acts 21:17–26.]] James and the elders told him that many Jews had heard that Paul taught the Jews of the Diaspora to abandon the law of Moses and the customs of their fathers. To put an end to the rumor, James told Paul to join four men under a vow, undergo the rite of purification in the Temple with them and pay the cost of their offerings.

{{kjv:Acts 21:20-26}}

{{fig:temple-model|The Second Temple in the model of first-century Jerusalem at the Israel Museum. In its courts Paul purified himself with four men under a vow, to show that he walked orderly and kept the law (Acts 21:24–26).}}

Without protest, Paul followed the instruction and pretended to keep the law. <em>De jure</em> he kept it that day in the Temple; <em>de facto</em> he had already abolished it in his letters, where he states repeatedly that the Torah is finished and circumcision with it.[[fn:cf. Galatians 3:13, 5:2–3; Colossians 2:14; Romans 6:14.]] The act shows that Paul was willing to adjust his rhetoric to context, and beyond that, to perform a ritual he rejected to win the trust of the local Jewish community. Small wonder that some accuse him of playing the Jew before Jews and the Gentile before Gentiles,[[fn:See 1 Corinthians 9:20–21.]] the <em>modus operandi</em> he describes himself in 1 Corinthians 9, and a moral relativism that opens the door wide to manipulation of the teaching.

That extreme interpretation set off a worsening tension between Paul and the disciples. Soon afterward, the personal tension between Barnabas and Paul reached its peak.

## The parting with Barnabas

Acts relates that Barnabas wanted to bring John, called Mark, with them. Paul disagreed, because Mark had left them in Pamphylia.[[fn:Acts 15:37–39.]] The quarrel drove them to part and go their separate ways.[[fn:Acts 15:35–41.]] Acts records:

{{kjv:Acts 15:39-40}}

The quarrel between Barnabas and Paul reflects a basic difference in their approach to the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. Barnabas held to the original message of <em>tawḥīd</em> and the law, as preserved by the <em>Urgemeinde</em>, the original community of the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam in Islamicjerusalem, led by James and supported by Peter. Paul leaned ever further toward adapting the teaching to pagan culture to attract Gentile followers. That change was a theological deviation, and it ended in a great split in the body of the early church.

Peter, regarded as the chief leader of the disciples, stressed obedience to the Jewish law. Acts records his preaching:

That approach differs from Paul's looser attitude to the obligations of Jewish law.[[fn:Galatians 2:16.]]

## Open conflict with Peter

At the same time Paul was in open dispute with Cephas (Peter), as his letter to the Galatians records:

{{kjv:Galatians 2:11-14}}

The passage reflects the basic tension between Paul's teaching, which leaned toward the Gentiles, and the teaching of the disciples, who held to the law of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, including obedience to the Torah. The disciples rejected Paul's theological adaptation because it undermined the foundation of <em>tawḥīd</em> and the authenticity of the message of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

At first Paul's call found no welcome among Gentiles, because of their hatred of the Jews. For that reason Paul began to distance himself from Barnabas and set his mind on spreading the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam to the Gentiles on his own terms.
