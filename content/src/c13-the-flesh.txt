The first two articles on Paul's character gathered his perjury, his curses, his hatreds and the order he prescribed for slaves and subjects. This one turns to the flesh: what he confessed of his own body, what his letters reveal of his preoccupations, the licence his doctrine gave, and what the earliest witnesses and the latest readers have made of it. His contempt for marriage and the thorn in his flesh are examined under [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"]Count·IV[/paulus_link], and are cited here without repetition.[[fn:1 Corinthians 7:1–9; 2 Corinthians 12:7. See [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"].]]

One limit governs the whole article. No source, hostile or friendly, records a sexual act of Paul's. What the record holds is his own account of a war inside his body, the vocabulary in which he fought it, the doctrine of licence he preached to others, and one ancient accusation about his motives. The deviance is documented in the mind and in the teaching. The reader will judge what it implies of the life.

## The war within

Paul describes himself as a man at war with his own members. The law of his mind is at war with the law in his members, which brings him into captivity to the law of sin; the good he would do, he does not; the evil he would not, he does.[[fn:Romans 7:15–24, quoted in full under Count·V; see [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament"].]] He tells the Corinthians that he beats his body and brings it into subjection, lest having preached to others he should himself be a castaway.[[fn:1 Corinthians 9:27.]]

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 9:27}}

The Greek is <em>hypōpiazō</em> (ὑπωπιάζω), to strike under the eye, to bruise. Whatever Paul's body wanted, he answered it with blows. To the Galatians he generalized the war into doctrine: the flesh lusts against the spirit and the spirit against the flesh, so that ye cannot do the things that ye would.[[fn:Galatians 5:17.]] A man who cannot do what he would, and who bruises his body to keep it from doing what it would, has told the reader as much as he can bear to.

## The vocabulary of lust

The same man returns, letter after letter, to catalogues of the sins of the body. To the Romans he describes at length the vile affections of women who changed the natural use, and of men who burned in their lust one toward another, "men with men working that which is unseemly."[[fn:Romans 1:24–27.]]

{{kjv:Romans 1:26-27}}

To the Corinthians he lists fornicators, idolaters, adulterers, "effeminate," and "abusers of themselves with mankind" among those who shall not inherit the kingdom, and adds: "and such were some of you."[[fn:1 Corinthians 6:9–11. The two Greek terms are <em>malakoi</em> (μαλακοί) and <em>arsenokoitai</em> (ἀρσενοκοῖται), the second a word first attested in Paul.]] To the Galatians the works of the flesh begin with adultery, fornication, uncleanness and lasciviousness.[[fn:Galatians 5:19–21.]] The gospels record ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) condemning lust once, in a sentence about the eye and the heart.[[fn:Matthew 5:28.]] Paul could not leave the subject alone. The word <em>arsenokoitai</em>, which no Greek writer before him used, is his coinage; the preoccupation is his signature.

## Licence

The paradox of Paul's doctrine is that the man who bruised his body preached that everything was permitted. "All things are lawful unto me," he tells the Corinthians twice; nothing is unclean of itself; "unto the pure all things are pure."[[fn:1 Corinthians 6:12; 10:23; Romans 14:14; Titus 1:15, the last from his school.]]

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 6:12}}

Al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār named the consequence a thousand years ago: Paul "opened for the people the door of permissiveness," told them circumcision was not required, the Sabbath not binding and wine lawful, and made their customs into a religion.[[fn:al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār, <em>Tathbīt Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa</em>, in Reynolds and Samir (eds.), <em>Critique of Christian Origins</em> (Provo, Utah: Brigham Young University Press, 2010), p. 183; quoted in full under [paulus_link slug="the-early-islamic-record"].]] The Arabic is <em>ibāḥah</em> (إباحة, "permissiveness"), the doctrine that what the law forbade is allowed.

## The fruit at Corinth

The door, once opened, admitted what one would expect. The church Paul founded at Corinth, and instructed that all things were lawful, produced within a few years a case of incest "such as is not so much as named among the Gentiles, that one should have his father's wife," and the congregation was puffed up about it.[[fn:1 Corinthians 5:1–2.]]

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 5:1-2}}

{{fig:corinth-apollo|The Temple of Apollo, Ancient Corinth. The church Paul founded in this city, taught that all things were lawful, tolerated within a few years the incest he describes in 1 Corinthians 5.}}

Members of the same church were going to prostitutes, and Paul had to ask them whether they did not know that he who is joined to a harlot is one body with her.[[fn:1 Corinthians 6:15–16.]] At the Lord's supper, one was hungry and another was drunken.[[fn:1 Corinthians 11:21.]] The letter that says "all things are lawful" is the letter that has to deal with incest, prostitution and drunkenness in the assembly that believed it. The fruit is the test, and these are the fruits of the first church built on Paul's doctrine.

## The oldest accusation

The community that James led kept a memory of Paul's motives that the church later suppressed. Epiphanius, writing against the Ebionites in the fourth century, records their tradition from a text called the Ascents of James: that Paul was a Gentile of Tarsus who converted to Judaism to marry the daughter of the high priest, was circumcised for her sake, and when refused, turned in anger against circumcision, the Sabbath and the law.[[fn:Epiphanius of Salamis, <em>Panarion</em> 30.16.8–9; see [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus"] and [paulus_link slug="the-first-witnesses"]. The text Epiphanius cites no longer survives.]]

The story cannot be verified and Epiphanius reports it to refute it. What it establishes is that the earliest witnesses, the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam who kept his law, explained Paul's apostasy from the law by a thwarted desire. They saw, at the root of his doctrine, a woman he could not have.

{{fig:epiphanius-ohrid|Epiphanius of Salamis, fresco by Michael and Eutychios in the Church of the Theotokos Peribleptos, Ohrid. The bishop who preserved the Ebionite account of Paul's motives recorded it to refute it.}}

## The modern readings

Modern readers have looked at the same evidence, the war within, the bruised body, the vocabulary, the thorn, the horror of marriage, and reached a conclusion the Ebionites did not. John Shelby Spong, an Anglican bishop, argued that Paul was a homosexual man who never acted on his desire and spent his life at war with it: "A rigidly controlled gay male, I believe, taught the church what the love of God means." He added that he saw "no evidence to suggest that Paul ever acted out his sexual desires and passions," and that nothing else could account for Paul's self-judging rhetoric, his negative feeling toward his own body, and his sense of being controlled by something he had no power to change.[[fn:John Shelby Spong, <em>Rescuing the Bible from Fundamentalism: A Bishop Rethinks the Meaning of Scripture</em> (San Francisco: Harper, 1991), in the chapter on Paul. Spong credits the suggestion to Arthur Darby Nock's <em>St. Paul</em> (1938).]]

The book on which this site rests cites the same possibility from Ehrman and Engberg-Pedersen, and adds the act of circumcising the adult Timothy with his own hands, by a man who had abolished circumcision, as a further shadow over his orientation and motives.[[fn:Acts 16:3; see [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"], with Bart D. Ehrman, <em>Peter, Paul and Mary Magdalene</em> (New York: Oxford University Press, 2006) and Troels Engberg-Pedersen, <em>Paul and the Stoics</em> (Edinburgh: T&amp;T Clark, 2000).]] Whether the desire Paul bruised was for women or for men, the readings agree on the war, and on its issue: a theology in which the body is the enemy and marriage a concession to weakness.

## The Islamic measure

Islam names the body a trust and desire a part of the <em>fiṭrah</em> (فطرة, "the natural constitution") on which God created humankind, to be governed by law and satisfied in marriage.[[fn:Sūrah al-Rūm 30:30.]] The Qurʾān commands the believers to marry the unmarried among them, and names among the signs of God that He made mates for them, that they might find rest in them, with affection and mercy between them.[[fn:Sūrah al-Nūr 24:32; Sūrah al-Rūm 30:21.]]

<blockquote><p class="arabic" lang="ar" dir="rtl">وَمِنْ ءَايَـٰتِهِۦٓ أَنْ خَلَقَ لَكُم مِّنْ أَنفُسِكُمْ أَزْوَٰجًا لِّتَسْكُنُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهَا وَجَعَلَ بَيْنَكُم مَّوَدَّةً وَرَحْمَةً</p><p>"And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy."[[fn:Sūrah al-Rūm 30:21, Saheeh International.]]</p></blockquote>

Of the monasticism that grew from Paul's theology of the body, the Qurʾān says that they invented it; God did not prescribe it for them.[[fn:Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:27.]] The prophets married, and the last of them said that whoever turned away from his practice was none of his.[[fn:Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5063.]] Paul bruised his body, loathed marriage, licensed everything and coined a word for the sin he could not stop naming. The Qurʾān's measure finds, in that record, a man at war with the constitution God gave him; and a man at war with the <em>fiṭrah</em> is no guide to it.
