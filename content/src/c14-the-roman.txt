Paul carried two names, spoke three languages and held a citizenship that most of the men he preached to could never obtain. [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus"]The profile of Paul[/paulus_link] sets out what the sources allow about his birth, his family and his training.[[fn:See [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus"].]] This article follows the Roman in him: how the name, the tongues and the citizenship were used, and what their use reveals.

## Two names

Acts calls him Saul for twelve chapters and Paul for the rest. The change is made in a single verse, on Cyprus, in the house of the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus: "Then Saul, (who also is called Paul,)."[[fn:Acts 13:9; cf. Acts 13:7 for the proconsul.]] From that verse the Hebrew name disappears from the narrative, and the Latin name that matched the governor's remains.

{{kjv:Acts 13:7-9}}

The coincidence is in the text. Luke, who arranged the narrative, chose to record the change of name at the moment his hero won his first Roman official. A man who had been Shaʾul of the tribe of Benjamin, named for Israel's first king, became Paulus, "the small one," in the presence of a Paulus who governed a province. The name he kept was the name that opened Roman doors.

## Three tongues

Before the Roman garrison in Islamicjerusalem, Paul asked the chief captain in Greek whether he might speak, and the captain, surprised, asked whether he was not the Egyptian who had led four thousand assassins into the wilderness.[[fn:Acts 21:37–38.]] Given leave, Paul turned to the crowd and spoke in Hebrew, and Luke notes that the crowd fell silent on hearing it.[[fn:Acts 21:40; 22:2.]]

{{kjv:Acts 21:37-40}}

The scene shows the instrument at work. Greek for the officer, Hebrew for the crowd, and a Roman name for the record. ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) spoke Aramaic to Galilean fishermen; the gospels preserve his words in that tongue.[[fn:Mark 5:41; 7:34; 15:34. See [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament"].]] Paul spoke whatever the room required, and the room was often Roman.

The orator of these scenes belongs to Acts. There Paul opens his defence before governors with the courtesies of a trained speaker, and stretches out his hand as the schools of rhetoric taught.[[fn:Acts 24:10; 26:1–3.]] His letters report what his hearers in Corinth said of him: that his letters were weighty and powerful, "but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible"; and he concedes that he is "rude in speech."[[fn:2 Corinthians 10:10; 11:6.]] Martin read the concession as historical evidence that Paul, by the standards of his day, was a poor public speaker, and the polished Paul of Acts as the author's portrait.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, notes on "Luke" and Paul, 2019, "Paul as Public Speaker and Rhetorician," published on this site with his permission: [paulus_link slug="dale-b-martin-luke-versus-paul" anchor="m-rhetorician" /].]]

## The citizenship

Twice in Acts Paul produces his citizenship at the moment of punishment. At Philippi, beaten and imprisoned without trial, he waits until the magistrates order his release and then refuses to go quietly: they have beaten Romans uncondemned, and they must come themselves to fetch him out.[[fn:Acts 16:37–39.]] In Islamicjerusalem, bound for scourging, he asks the centurion whether it is lawful to scourge a Roman uncondemned; the chief captain, who bought his own citizenship at great price, learns that Paul was born to it, and is afraid.[[fn:Acts 22:25–29.]]

{{kjv:Acts 22:25-28}}

Roman citizenship in the provinces of the first century was rare among Jews and could be inherited only from a citizen father.[[fn:Paul's father is mentioned in no reliable source; on his citizenship see [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus"], and Robert Eisenman, "Paul as Herodian," <em>Journal of Higher Criticism</em> 3, no. 1 (1996): 110–122.]] Paul possessed it, and he used it as a shield against the discipline of the synagogue and the rod of the magistrate alike. The disciples of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam had no such shield. Peter and John were beaten by the Sanhedrin and rejoiced that they were counted worthy to suffer.[[fn:Acts 5:40–41.]] Stephen was stoned with Saul consenting.[[fn:Acts 7:58–8:1.]] James, the brother of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, was stoned in 62 at the order of the high priest Ananus.[[fn:Flavius Josephus, <em>Antiquities of the Jews</em> 20.9.1.]] None of them could say "I am a Roman."

## The appeal to Caesar

The shield had a final use. Before Festus at Caesarea, offered a trial in Islamicjerusalem, Paul refused the jurisdiction of the Sanhedrin altogether: "I appeal unto Caesar."[[fn:Acts 25:10–12.]] Festus answered that to Caesar he should go, and Agrippa remarked afterward that the man might have been set at liberty had he not appealed.[[fn:Acts 26:32.]]

{{kjv:Acts 25:11-12}}

The appeal took the case out of the hands of the people to whom ʿĪsā ibn Maryam was sent and placed it in the hands of the power that had condemned him. Paul chose Rome over Islamicjerusalem as his judge, and Rome, in the end, judged him: the tradition that he died under Nero is examined in [paulus_link slug="the-failed-prophet"]Count·VI[/paulus_link].[[fn:See [paulus_link slug="the-failed-prophet"].]] Robert Eisenman reads the whole pattern, the citizenship, the appeal, the doctrine of obedience to the powers, as the politics of a man aligned with the Herodian and Roman order against the people who resisted it.[[fn:Eisenman, op. cit., pp. 115ff.; cf. Romans 13:1–7, treated under [paulus_link slug="women-slaves-and-caesar"]The character[/paulus_link].]]

Paul stands at the right hand of the viewer, already bald and bearded in the type the church would fix for him, honoured in the city whose citizenship he claimed.}}

## What the Roman shows

The Qurʾān describes the messengers as men of their own people, sent in the tongue of their people so that the message might be plain.[[fn:Sūrah Ibrāhīm 14:4.]] ʿĪsā ibn Maryam was sent to the Children of Israel, spoke their tongue and died to the world in their land. Paul was a citizen of the empire that ruled them, took its name, spoke its language when it served, invoked its protection when threatened and appealed to its emperor when tried. The religion he founded followed the same road, from Islamicjerusalem to Rome, and made its home there. That is the subject of [paulus_link slug="paul-the-father-of-a-new-religion"]Count·VI[/paulus_link].
