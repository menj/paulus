Paul carried two names, spoke three languages and held a citizenship that most of the men he preached to could never obtain. [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus"]The profile of Paul[/paulus_link] sets out what the sources allow about his birth, his family and his training.[[fn:See [paulus_link slug="who-was-paul-of-tarsus" /].]] This article follows the Roman in him in two parts: the name and the tongues here, then the citizenship and the appeal to Caesar, and what their use reveals.

## Two names

Acts calls him Saul for twelve chapters and Paul for the rest. The change is made in a single verse, on Cyprus, in the house of the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus: "Then Saul, (who also is called Paul,)."[[fn:Acts 13:9; cf. Acts 13:7 for the proconsul.]] From that verse the Hebrew name disappears from the narrative, and the Latin name that matched the governor's remains.

{{kjv:Acts 13:7-9}}

The coincidence is in the text. Luke, who arranged the narrative, chose to record the change of name at the moment his hero won his first Roman official. A man who had been Shaʾul of the tribe of Benjamin, named for Israel's first king, became Paulus, "the small one," in the presence of a Paulus who governed a province. The name he kept was the name that opened Roman doors.

## Three tongues

Before the Roman garrison in Islamicjerusalem, Paul asked the chief captain in Greek whether he might speak, and the captain, surprised, asked whether he was not the Egyptian who had led four thousand assassins into the wilderness.[[fn:Acts 21:37–38.]] Given leave, Paul turned to the crowd and spoke in Hebrew, and Luke notes that the crowd fell silent on hearing it.[[fn:Acts 21:40; 22:2.]]

{{kjv:Acts 21:37-40}}

The scene shows the instrument at work. Greek for the officer, Hebrew for the crowd, and a Roman name for the record. ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) spoke Aramaic to Galilean fishermen; the gospels preserve his words in that tongue.[[fn:Mark 5:41; 7:34; 15:34. See [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament" /].]] Paul spoke whatever the room required, and the room was often Roman.

The orator of these scenes belongs to Acts. There Paul opens his defence before governors with the courtesies of a trained speaker, and stretches out his hand as the schools of rhetoric taught.[[fn:Acts 24:10; 26:1–3.]] His letters report what his hearers in Corinth said of him: that his letters were weighty and powerful, "but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible"; and he concedes that he is "rude in speech."[[fn:2 Corinthians 10:10; 11:6.]] Martin read the concession as historical evidence that Paul, by the standards of his day, was a poor public speaker, and the polished Paul of Acts as the author's portrait.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, "‘Luke’ versus Paul," unpublished notes toward a book, written 18 March 2019 and revised 31 May and 5 June 2019, sent to the author in early 2023, section "Paul as Public Speaker and Rhetorician," published on this site with his permission: [paulus_link slug="dale-b-martin-luke-versus-paul" anchor="m-rhetorician" /].]]
