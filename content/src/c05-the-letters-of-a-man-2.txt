Paul's immersion in Greek culture and philosophy (Hellenism) and in Stoicism, which saw nature as divine, made it easier for him to draw the people of the time into the new religion he brought. Some of these schools rejected the desires of the body and made restraint of appetite the mark of spiritual purity.[[fn:See John Dillon, <em>The Middle Platonists: 80 B.C. to A.D. 220</em> (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 1996); and Troels Engberg-Pedersen, <em>Paul and the Stoics</em> (Edinburgh: T&amp;T Clark, 2000).]] The dualism of spirit (πνεῦμα, <em>pneuma</em>) and body (σάρξ, <em>sarx</em>) was the basis of Greek thought, in which the body was the source of weakness and sin while the spirit was pure and noble.

Small wonder that Paul inherited the tendency. He states plainly that "it is good for a man not to touch a woman." He repeats it in verse 7, where he regards celibacy as the better, even the ideal state. Yet in the same passage he advises every man to have his own wife to avoid fornication.[[fn:1 Corinthians 7:9.]]

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 7:1-2}}

The contradiction raises a question: is marriage a good to be encouraged, or a compromise to avoid sin? The ambiguity shows an ethical confusion in Paul's teaching on marriage and sex. Sexual desire is treated as a weakness to be avoided altogether, and never as part of human nature to be governed responsibly.[[fn:See James D. G. Dunn, <em>The Theology of Paul the Apostle</em> (Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1998).]]

Paul also defended his right to take a wife like the other apostles,[[fn:1 Corinthians 9:5.]] although he rejected marriage as a personal choice.[[fn:1 Corinthians 7:6–7.]] That adds to the confusion: on one side he distrusts marriage as a hindrance to full devotion to God,[[fn:1 Corinthians 7:32–34.]] on the other he acknowledges the institution as a right and a practice of the disciples. The mixed attitude reveals an inner tension between the ascetic impulse and real social need. A teaching of this kind is foreign to every earlier moral and ethical tradition, and it reflects a deep discomfort with the body and with human desire.

Paul's view runs directly against the Talmudic tradition, which recognizes marriage and intimacy as a sacred duty and a source of happiness. In the Talmud, marital relations are an obligation to be fulfilled with responsibility and love. The tragic story of R. Rehumi, who neglected his wife in pursuit of learning until it led to his death,[[fn:See Kethuboth 62b.]] stresses the importance of tending the marriage. The saying that "a woman prefers one <em>kab</em>[[fn:The <em>kab</em> (קַב) is an ancient unit of volume in the Jewish system of measures, about 1.2 to 1.3 liters, used chiefly for dry goods in Talmudic tradition.]] with gaiety to ten <em>kab</em> with abstinence" asserts the priority of domestic happiness over a life hemmed in by restriction or the denial of pleasure.

The Talmudic tradition goes further and gives priority to the duties of family and offspring. Even a man who has fulfilled the commandment to be fruitful should not live without a wife.[[fn:Yevamoth 63b.]] The tradition also condemns vows that keep husband and wife apart, as something that can destroy the harmony of the home.[[fn:Nedarim 82a.]] In the Jewish view intimacy is a natural part of life, to be governed wisely and responsibly.

## The undefiled bed

The same preference reaches its fullest image at the end of the canon, where the Apocalypse honours 144,000 men "which were not defiled with women; for they are virgins."[[fn:Revelation 14:4. Michael Goulder assigns the ideal of this verse to the church of Jerusalem, and still grants that Paul "had the gift of celibate continence and wished others did too." Michael Goulder, <em>St. Paul versus St. Peter: A Tale of Two Missions</em> (Louisville: Westminster John Knox Press, 1995), pp. 65–67.]]

Another book of the same canon answers it. The Epistle to the Hebrews declares: "Marriage is honourable in all, and the bed undefiled."[[fn:Hebrews 13:4.]] Its adjective, <em>amiantos</em> (ἀμίαντος), negates the very defilement the Apocalypse assigns to women. Hebrews honours the marriage bed; Paul ranked it second, a remedy for those who burn.

{{kjv:Hebrews 13:4}}

A letter written in Paul's own name goes further. It warns that "in the latter times some shall depart from the faith, giving heed to seducing spirits, and doctrines of devils," and the first doctrine it names is "forbidding to marry."[[fn:1 Timothy 4:1–3. The Pastoral Epistles are the work of Paul's school; see [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament" /].]] Paul permitted marriage as a concession. The premise behind the concession, that the man who keeps from women stands nearer to God, is the premise every prohibition of marriage requires, and his own school called that prohibition demonic.

The premise exacted its price early. The Gospel of Matthew praises those who "have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake,"[[fn:Matthew 19:12.]] and Eusebius records that the young Origen took the words "in too literal and extreme a sense" and castrated himself, and that his bishop at first admired the act.[[fn:Eusebius, <em>Church History</em> 6.8.1–3, trans. Arthur Cushman McGiffert, <em>Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers</em>, 2nd ser., vol. 1; cf. Goulder, op. cit., p. 65.]]

The Qurʾān traces the ascetic turn to the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him): "monasticism, which they innovated; We did not prescribe it for them."[[fn:Qurʾān, al-Ḥadīd 57:27 (Sahih International).]] It counts marriage among the signs of God, with <em>mawaddah wa raḥmah</em> (affection and mercy) placed between husband and wife.[[fn:Qurʾān, al-Rūm 30:21.]] When three Companions resolved on the celibate life, the Prophet Muhammad (ṣallā Allāh ʿalayhi wa-sallam, God bless him and grant him peace) answered them: "I fast and break my fast, I do sleep and I also marry women. So he who does not follow my tradition in religion, is not from me."[[fn:Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5063, Kitāb al-Nikāḥ.]] When ʿUthmān ibn Maẓʿūn asked to renounce marriage, the Prophet refused, and Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ recorded what would otherwise have followed: "if he had allowed him, we would have gotten ourselves castrated."[[fn:Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5073, Kitāb al-Nikāḥ.]]

In Alexandria a young teacher castrated himself for the kingdom, and his bishop admired him. In Madinah the Prophet forbade it, and the matter ended there.

## The thorn in the flesh

Paul describes himself as one "given a thorn in the flesh" (σκόλοψ τῇ σαρκί, <em>skólops tē sarki</em>),[[fn:2 Corinthians 12:7.]] a mysterious phrase much interpreted by scholars. Some connect it with physical illness; others read it as a figure for uncontrolled desire. If it refers to sexual desire, that would explain Paul's insistence on celibacy as the way out.

{{kjv:2 Corinthians 12:7-9}}

A more detailed clinical hypothesis comes from Antonio Pascual, Anna Manresa Lamarca and Josep Piquero Casals, who propose that the phrase refers to a chronic skin disease, painful and socially repellent. On dermatological and historical grounds they suggest that Paul may have suffered from lupus, dermatomyositis or nodular vasculitis, diseases that bear directly on body image and social interaction, so that the suffering was both outward and psychological.[[fn:See Pascual et al., "Saint Paul's Thorn in the Flesh: A Dermatological Weakness?," <em>Acta Medico-Historica Adriatica</em>, vol. 20, no. 1 (2022), pp. 83–98.]] That reading also opens the possibility that the physical suffering aggravated an existing inner conflict, including a struggle against the urges of the body.

Paul's own statements support it: bodily weakness, continual suffering, and a disease he saw as an affliction from Satan (ἄγγελος Σατανᾶ). In his letters Paul often presents the body as the source of weakness and sin. He ties bodily desire to "the flesh," which he sets against "the spirit."[[fn:See Galatians 5:17; Romans 8:5–8.]] For Paul, the superior spiritual life demands total mastery of bodily impulse, a dualism drawn from Hellenistic philosophy, in which the body is the prison of the soul.

Some modern scholars have raised the possibility that Paul's attitude sprang from unresolved sexual conflict or repressed sexual desires never consciously addressed. His extreme severity toward sexual relations and the founding of families, and his failure to acknowledge the place of physical love in religious life, raise questions about the psychological background and the real orientation that may have lain hidden.[[fn:See Bart D. Ehrman, <em>Peter, Paul and Mary Magdalene: The Followers of Jesus in History and Legend</em> (New York: Oxford University Press, 2006); also Troels Engberg-Pedersen, op. cit.]]

His act of circumcising Timothy with his own hands[[fn:Acts 16:3.]] only deepens the strange shadow over his orientation and motives. A grown man circumcising a young man for political reasons is no neutral act, above all when done by a figure who rejected the law and yet chose to carry it out himself. Within a teaching so full of hostility to sex, the body and women, the incident gives a faint signal of a dark side of Paul rarely discussed openly: an inner doubt about his own identity, perhaps too deep to admit consciously.

Paul's contempt for intimacy and his elevation of celibacy as a higher form of spirituality reflect an inner conflict. Behind the spiritual idealism he tried to defend lay fear and rejection of bodily desire, which he regarded as a threat to purity. If so, the ideology of celibacy inherited from Paul's teaching reflects an unbalanced personal experience. From it grew a theology of the body that distrusts desire, and from that the institutions of the celibate priesthood and monasticism, a spirituality founded on rejection of the human body.[[fn:Walter Burkert, <em>Ancient Mystery Cults</em> (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1987).]]

Letters of this kind show a man, with his errands, his ailments and his fears. The claim that sets them apart as revelation rests on other grounds: the witnesses Paul called and the visions he reported. The next article weighs them.
