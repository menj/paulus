Rejection of Paul's standing as an apostle runs through oral history and extra-canonical tradition, and it is also implied in the text of the New Testament itself. One passage often cited is the message to the church of Ephesus in the book of Revelation,[[fn:The book of Revelation belongs to the apocalyptic genre and is composed as symbolic vision. It is no linear historical record, but carries prophetic symbolism that reflects conflict within the early church. See Elaine Pagels, <em>Revelations: Visions, Prophecy, and Politics in the Book of Revelation</em> (Viking, 2012), pp. 5ff.]] where the author, known as John, delivers a message attributed to Jesus:

{{kjv:Revelation 2:2}}

{{fig:celsus-library|The Library of Celsus, Ephesus. Revelation praises the church of this city for testing those who say they are apostles and finding them liars (Revelation 2:2).}}

The passage is striking because it is addressed to the church of Ephesus, a community Paul himself founded and the recipient of a letter in his name, which calls him an apostle of Christ. Although the verse names no one, its context leaves room to understand that claims of apostleship in the early churches were not accepted without question. A letter written in his name has him complain that all Asia had turned away from him, including those who had once supported him.[[fn:2 Timothy 1:15.]] That coincidence has led some modern scholars to reassess Paul's relationship with the churches of Asia, including the possibility that he was among those tested and rejected as a false apostle by the very community he had formed.

Some modern interpreters within Christian apologetics try to rule out that possibility by stressing that the statement in Revelation is plural and unspecific. The "false apostles," they argue, could refer to other figures who appeared after the apostolic age. They add that if the Ephesian church ever rejected false teaching, it was the fruit of Paul's own original instruction, which had built up its faith. That is a <em>petitio principii</em>: Paul's authority is assumed to credit him with the church's discernment. That interpretation denies that the rebuke in Revelation carries any implication for Paul. It ignores the historical reality that the early church did not agree on accepting anyone's authority as an apostle, least of all a figure outside the circle of <em>ḥawāriyyūn</em> known directly to ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

Historians and textual scholars such as Robert Eisenman,[[fn:Robert Eisenman, <em>James the Brother of Jesus</em> (Penguin Books, 1997), pp. 626–628.]] James D. Tabor[[fn:James D. Tabor, <em>Paul and Jesus: How the Apostle Transformed Christianity</em> (Simon &amp; Schuster, 2012).]] and Elaine Pagels[[fn:Elaine Pagels, op. cit., pp. 25–53.]] have proposed that Revelation was written in a context of internal tension in the early church, above all between the original disciples and the outside influence that began to seep in through Paul's movement. They read the rejection of false apostles in the text as an allusion to those who brought teaching that departed from the original tradition inherited by the disciples of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

Eisenman stresses that the movement of the disciples centered on James had firm reason to question Paul's "apostleship". Pagels argues that the author of Revelation opposed practices that permitted eating food offered to idols and mixing with Roman custom, two matters that connect to the flexibility of Paul's teaching in his letters.[[fn:ibid., p. 30. Pagels states that John condemns figures who permitted ritual compromise with the pagan world.]] Although Paul is never named, suspicion of apostolic authority from outside the circle of the <em>ḥawāriyyūn</em> is plain, and it shows that rejection of Paul came from within the early texts of the church as well as from outside them.
