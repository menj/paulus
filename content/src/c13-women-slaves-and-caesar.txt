The first article on Paul's character gathered what his own letters add to the record of his conduct. This one turns to the moral order he prescribed for others. His rule of silence for women, and its contradiction by his own practice, is set out under [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament"]Count·V[/paulus_link]; his distrust of marriage and the body under [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"]Count·IV[/paulus_link].[[fn:1 Corinthians 14:34–35; 1 Corinthians 7:1–9. See [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament"] and [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"].]] What follows is what those pages leave out.

## Women, by the school of Paul

The rule of silence hardened, in a letter written in Paul's name, into doctrine: a woman is to learn in silence with all subjection, may not teach or hold authority over a man, because Adam was formed first and the woman was deceived; she shall be saved in childbearing.[[fn:1 Timothy 2:11–15. The Pastoral Epistles are the work of Paul's school; see [paulus_link slug="paul-and-the-new-testament" anchor="s-two-pauls-the-man-and-the-canon"]Count·V[/paulus_link]. The rule is his own, from 1 Corinthians 14; the school supplied the theology.]]

{{kjv:1 Timothy 2:11-15}}

Bernard Shaw, no Muslim and no churchman, drew the conclusion from Paul's view of the wife as a release from desire: it "has made Paul the eternal enemy of Woman."[[fn:Bernard Shaw, "Preface on the Prospects of Christianity," in <em>Androcles and the Lion, Overruled, Pygmalion</em> (London: Constable, 1916), in the sections on Paul.]] The Qurʾān names the mother of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) as chosen above the women of the worlds.[[fn:Sūrah Āl ʿImrān 3:42.]] The rule of silence is Paul's, and the churches that kept it kept his.

## Slaves

Paul never condemned slavery. He told the slave who came to the faith to "care not for it," allowing only that he might take his freedom if it came.[[fn:1 Corinthians 7:21.]] When a slave named Onesimus fled to him, he sent the man back to his owner Philemon, with a letter asking for kindness and offering to pay any debt.[[fn:Philemon 1:10–19.]] The letters written in his name went further, and commanded slaves to obey their masters with fear and trembling, as they would obey Christ.[[fn:Ephesians 6:5–8; Colossians 3:22–24.]]

{{kjv:Ephesians 6:5}}

The letter to Philemon is often praised as tender. It is a letter returning a human being to the man who owned him. Bishop Spong listed "servants, obey your masters" among the commands that reflect a first-century prejudice and become, when defended as scripture, a moral burden.[[fn:John Shelby Spong, <em>Rescuing the Bible from Fundamentalism: A Bishop Rethinks the Meaning of Scripture</em> (San Francisco: Harper, 1991), p. 101.]]

## Caesar

To the Romans, Paul wrote that every soul is to be subject to the higher powers, that the powers that be are ordained of God, and that whoever resists them resists the ordinance of God. The ruler bears the sword as the minister of God.[[fn:Romans 13:1–4.]]

{{kjv:Romans 13:1-2}}

{{fig:nero-bust|Portrait bust of Nero. The powers Paul told the Romans were ordained of God were his.}}

The ruler in question was Nero. Spong names the verse beside the rule of silence and the rule of slaves as the crust of the first century that must be broken off before Paul's letters can be read at all.[[fn:Spong, op. cit., p. 101.]] The Verdict sets out where the doctrine of obedience led.

## The witnesses of conscience

Three men who owed nothing to Islam reached the same verdict on Paul's character from his own writings. Thomas Jefferson wrote to William Short in 1820 that of the "band of dupes and impostors, Paul was the great Coryphaeus, and first corrupter of the doctrines of Jesus."[[fn:Thomas Jefferson to William Short, 13 April 1820, in <em>The Papers of Thomas Jefferson, Retirement Series</em>, vol. 15, pp. 538–541; Founders Online, National Archives.]] Bentham, three years later, wrote a book to separate the religion of the one from the religion of the other.[[fn:Bentham, <em>Not Paul, but Jesus</em> (London, 1823).]] Shaw, in 1915, found the outcome: "There has really never been a more monstrous imposition perpetrated than the imposition of the limitations of Paul's soul upon the soul of Jesus."[[fn:Shaw, op. cit., the sentence that closes his account of Paul's conversion.]]

Muslim scholarship reached the verdict first, on the same evidence. Al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār's account of Paul, given in [paulus_link slug="the-early-islamic-record"]The Islamic tradition[/paulus_link], is a moral account before it is a doctrinal one: a man who opened the door of permissiveness, abolished the law and sought the favour of Caesar.[[fn:al-Qāḍī ʿAbd al-Jabbār, <em>Tathbīt Dalāʾil al-Nubuwwa</em>, in Reynolds and Samir (eds.), <em>Critique of Christian Origins</em> (Provo, Utah: Brigham Young University Press, 2010), p. 183.]]

## The fruits

The test ʿĪsā ibn Maryam gave was of fruits, and the fruits are in the record. A man who lied for the gospel, swore a vow he had renounced in writing, cursed the disciples and wished them mutilated, reviled the high priest and pleaded ignorance, called the Jews contrary to all men, returned a slave, and blessed the sword of Nero. In Islam the prophets are protected from such conduct by <em>ʿiṣmah</em> (عصمة, "protection from sin"); a man who claims their office and shows this record has, <em>ipso facto</em>, disproved the claim.

The prophets whom the Qurʾān names are known for what they bore: Ayyūb his affliction, Yūsuf his prison, ʿĪsā ibn Maryam the plots of his own people, without a curse on any of them. Paul is known for what he inflicted. The Qurʾān's question stands: why do you say what you do not do?[[fn:Sūrah al-Ṣaff 61:2.]]
