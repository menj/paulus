The dominance reaches beyond the number of texts or the order of writing into the content and the doctrine. Paul's letters show internal contradictions that cannot be ignored, between the doctrine of one letter and another, and sometimes within the same letter. If a man claims to deliver the revelation of God, the consistency of his teaching is the <em>sine qua non</em> of the claim.

On justification, Paul writes to the Romans that the doers of the law shall be justified, and a chapter later that by the deeds of the law no flesh shall be justified. Both verses speak of the law itself, so the usual defence, that Paul sets the works of the law apart from good deeds in general, has nothing to hold on to here.

{{kjv:Romans 3:20}}

That creates a theological tension between faith and works, and leaves open whether the law still applies. On another matter he states in 1 Corinthians 6:11 that Christians have been sanctified and justified, but in Romans 7:19–24 he admits that he goes on struggling with a sin he cannot leave.

{{kjv:Romans 7:19-24}}

On the law, Paul insists that believers stand free of it[[fn:Romans 6:14.]] and that Christ is the end of the law.[[fn:Romans 10:4.]] Yet in Romans 3:31 and 7:12 he says the law is good and must be upheld.

{{kjv:Romans 3:31}}

{{kjv:Romans 7:12}}

The contradiction shows a basic uncertainty about the place of law in religious life.

The sharpest contradictions concern women. In 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 he states plainly that women must keep silent in the assembly and may say nothing. In 1 Corinthians 11:5 he permits women to prophesy and pray openly, provided they cover their heads. That is a basic inconsistency about the public role of women in the Christian assembly.

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 14:34-35}}

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 11:5}}

In Galatians 3:28 Paul declares that there is "neither male nor female" in Christ, as if endorsing absolute equality. To the Corinthians he writes that "the head of the woman is the man," and requires her to cover her head when she prays.[[fn:1 Corinthians 11:3–10.]] The letters written in his name carry the rule further: in Ephesians 5:22–24 wives are ordered to submit to their husbands because the husband is the "head" of the wife. The principle of Galatians does not survive either text.

More confusing still, Paul praises women such as Phoebe as a διάκονος (<em>diákonos</em>, minister of the church)[[fn:Romans 16:1–2.]] and calls Junia "of note among the apostles,"[[fn:Romans 16:7.]] but the first letter to Timothy, written in his name, forbids women to teach or hold authority over men.[[fn:1 Timothy 2:12.]] Once again, actual practice conflicts with the doctrinal rule he wants to enforce.

These contradictions are more than editorial slips or matters of style. What we have inherited as "holy epistles" is, read <em>in toto</em>, a set of mutually contradictory letters from the pen of a man tossed between personal inspiration and the desire to master the message of <em>tawḥīd</em> (توحيد, "the oneness of God") brought by the prophets.
