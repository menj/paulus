The first three doctrines concern God, the cross and faith. The last four concern the law and the people of the covenant.

## 4. The law abolished and cursed

Paul also taught that the law of the Torah ceased to be in force after the crucifixion of Jesus Christ at Golgotha. In his view the law of Mūsā was the source of sin and a form of curse, a teaching absent <em>ab initio</em> from the words of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, who kept the law and told his hearers to keep it.[[fn:Romans 4:15; Galatians 2:21, 3:10.]] When Jesus Christ was crucified, Paul taught, he "took the curse" of the law upon himself to free humanity from it. In an arrogant tone he also declared:

{{kjv:Galatians 5:4}}

Paul stressed, further, that Jesus Christ is the end of the law.[[fn:Romans 7:6, 10:4; Galatians 3:24–25.]]

Set these beside the words of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam in the Sermon on the Mount. Whoever breaks the least of the commandments, and teaches others to do the same, shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven:

{{kjv:Matthew 5:19}}

Paul did more than relax the least of the commandments. He told his congregations in writing that they were delivered from the law, and the letters that told them so became scripture.

Paul rejected the validity of the law as a condition of salvation, and he also symbolically degraded its worth. In his letter to the Philippians he states plainly:

{{kjv:Philippians 3:8}}

The Greek word <em>skubala</em> (σκύβαλα) used here is extremely coarse: it means excrement, refuse, filth. What Paul counts as refuse in this context is more than social or religious standing. It is the whole inheritance of the Torah on which Jewish identity rests, including his own status as a Pharisee and his obedience to the law. Implicitly, Paul places the law of the Torah in the symbolic category of "spiritual filth" to be cast off to receive what he claims as the knowledge of Christ. The statement shows how radically Paul broke continuity with the earlier law: he went beyond abolishing it in doctrine and demeaned its worth in theology and rhetoric.

Although Paul said he did not oppose the teachings of the Torah, in fact he made himself the chief opponent of its laws.[[fn:Compare Genesis 17:19; also Matthew 5:17.]]

Some scholars now read Paul as closing the law to Gentiles while leaving Jews free to keep it.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, "Paul as Jewish Theologian," Lecture 16, RLST 152: Introduction to New Testament History and Literature, Open Yale Courses, Yale University, Spring 2009, https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152.]] The reading rescues his consistency at the cost of his own words. Writing to those "that know the law," he tells them they are "delivered from the law."[[fn:Romans 7:1, 6.]] He says that he became as one under the law to win those under it, "not being myself under the law," though he was a Jew born and circumcised.[[fn:1 Corinthians 9:20, where the Greek reads μὴ ὢν αὐτὸς ὑπὸ νόμον (<em>mē ōn autos hypo nomon</em>), a phrase missing from the manuscripts behind the King James Version; Philippians 3:5.]] At Antioch he rebuked Peter, a Jew, for compelling Gentiles to keep what Peter himself no longer kept, and he counted his own life under the law "but dung."[[fn:Galatians 2:14; Philippians 3:5–8.]] A man who holds the law binding on Jews does not write of it in these terms.

## 5. Circumcision abolished

On circumcision, Paul stated:

{{kjv:Galatians 5:2}}

Although the word of God repeatedly stresses the need for real circumcision,[[fn:Genesis 17:13; Deuteronomy 10:16 and 30:6; Jeremiah 4:4.]] Paul boldly abolished the law of circumcision. That contradicts the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam, recorded in the New Testament itself, where he was circumcised.[[fn:Luke 2:21.]]

## 6. The food laws abolished

On the foods God had forbidden in the Torah, such as the flesh of swine, Paul gave his opinion:

{{kjv:Romans 14:14}}

{{kjv:1 Corinthians 10:25}}

That is why Luke, his pupil, "composed" a story in which God came to Peter in a dream and told him to eat every animal without exception, lawful or forbidden.[[fn:Acts 10:10–15.]]

## 7. The chosen people redefined

Paul interpreted the concept of the "chosen people," the elect, as open to every nation, which looks inclusive on the surface. His interpretation often contradicts the original teaching rooted in the Children of Israel as the people God chose to carry the message of <em>tawḥīd</em> (توحيد, "the oneness of God"). Paul says "there is neither Jew nor Greek."[[fn:Galatians 3:28.]] That contradicts the special role of the Children of Israel that ʿĪsā ibn Maryam affirmed:

{{kjv:Matthew 15:24}}

Paul's statement blurs the real meaning of "chosen" in the tradition of the prophets and reflects an effort to draw more Gentiles into his teaching, even at the price of abandoning a chief principle of earlier revelation.

From this analysis it is plain that Paul made sweeping changes to the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam to attract Gentiles. His stress on the concept of divinity and on the doctrine of salvation from sin shows the great part Paul played in shaping modern Christian theology. Where those doctrines came from, revelation or the schools of his day, is the fourth count, set out in [paulus_link slug="the-letters-of-a-man"]Count·IV[/paulus_link].
