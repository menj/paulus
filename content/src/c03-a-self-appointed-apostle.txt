Immediately after the event of his repentance, Saul began to claim that he had received "guidance" directly from Jesus Christ. He then sought out the disciples to declare his repentance for what he had done to them.[[fn:cf. Acts, chapters 9 to 13.]]

{{fig:bab-kisan|Bab Kisan, one of the old gates of Damascus, now housing the Chapel of Saint Paul, which marks the tradition that he escaped the city by night, let down the wall in a basket (Acts 9:25; 2 Corinthians 11:33).}}

The New Testament states that all the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) at first kept away from Saul, until Barnabas came forward to believe him and give him protection. Acts records it:

{{kjv:Acts 9:26-27}}

Through Barnabas, Saul was accepted by the other followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam and became known as Paul. On the strength of his repentance, Paul declared himself one of the disciples of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.

Paul's own account of the same years does not agree with Luke's. He tells the Galatians that after Damascus he went up to none of the apostles, went instead into Arabia and back to Damascus, and reached Jerusalem only after three years, where he stayed fifteen days with Peter and saw no other apostle except James:

{{kjv:Galatians 1:17-19}}

Acts has Barnabas bring him "to the apostles" as a new and doubted convert; Paul, who adds "before God, I lie not," says he met two of them, and only after three years of preaching on his own authority. The two accounts cannot both stand. Historians who set them side by side generally prefer Paul's letter to Luke's narrative, as the earlier source and the one written by Paul himself.[[fn:Galatians 1:20. On the comparison and its verdict, see Dale B. Martin, "The New Testament as History" and "Paul as Missionary," Lectures 5 and 14, RLST 152: Introduction to New Testament History and Literature, Open Yale Courses, Yale University, Spring 2009, https://oyc.yale.edu/religious-studies/rlst-152.]] Either way, the apostleship Paul claimed was established without the apostles.

The disagreement begins earlier, with where Paul was known at all. In Acts, Saul makes havoc of the church in Jerusalem, entering every house and dragging men and women to prison, before he sets out for Damascus.[[fn:Acts 8:3; 9:1–2.]] In his own letter, Paul says that when he first went up to Jerusalem, three years after his call, he was still "unknown by face unto the churches of Judaea."[[fn:Galatians 1:22.]] A persecutor who had raided the houses of the Jerusalem believers could hardly have been a stranger to their faces. Dale B. Martin, setting the two accounts side by side, judged that they cannot responsibly be harmonised: if Paul is telling the truth, the account in Acts cannot be historical.[[fn:Dale B. Martin, notes on "Luke" and Paul, 2019, "The Chronology and Geography of Paul’s “Conversion” and Early Ministry," published on this site with his permission: [paulus_link slug="dale-b-martin-luke-versus-paul" anchor="m-chronology" /].]]

Paul often, and without embarrassment, claimed to belong among the apostles, as in these words:

Paul's proclamation of himself as an apostle drew no attention from the Jewish community, and the followers of the mission of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam did not believe him at all. How could they? Paul had been the chief enemy of the followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. Not long before, he had been the one arresting, punishing and killing their brethren for their faith. The New Testament itself records that the main supporters of the mission of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam before Paul's appearance were Barnabas and Peter.

By his own admission, Paul never studied under ʿĪsā ibn Maryam and never heard any teaching from him. Yet because he claimed to belong among the apostles, the teaching he devised was crowned "the revelation of God."
