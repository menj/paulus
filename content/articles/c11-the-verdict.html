What the demographers project

The demographic record already shows the drift. In 2015 the Pew Research Center projected the world's religions from 2010 to 2050.

Christianity began the period as the largest religion, with an estimated 2.2 billion adherents, 31 per cent of the world's 6.9 billion people, while Islam stood second with 1.6 billion, or 23 per cent. By 2050 the projection brings the two to near parity, Muslims at 2.8 billion and Christians at 2.9 billion; extended further, the Muslim share equals the Christian share, at roughly 32 per cent each, around 2070, and exceeds it after that.4

[paulus_chart id="pew-share-2050"]

The losses come by conversion as well as by birth. About 40 million people are projected to switch into Christianity by 2050, and 106 million to leave it, the largest net loss of any religious group.5

[paulus_chart id="pew-switching"]

In Europe, long the heartland of Christendom, the Christian population is projected to fall from 553 million to 454 million, from three-quarters of the population to less than two-thirds.6

The same projections show the religion leaving its old ground. In 2010 a quarter of the world's Christians lived in Europe, and less than 1 per cent in the Middle East and North Africa, where Christianity began; by 2050 Europe's share is projected to fall to about 16 per cent, while sub-Saharan Africa's rises from 24 to 38 per cent.7

[paulus_chart id="pew-christian-regions"]

Pew's update of 2017 carried the projection to 2060. Between 2015 and 2060 the world's population is projected to grow by 32 per cent and the number of Muslims by 70 per cent, from 1.8 billion to nearly 3 billion, taking Muslims from 24.1 to 31.1 per cent of humanity.

The reasons Pew gives are demographic: Muslim women have 2.9 children on average, against 2.6 among Christians, and the median age of Muslims in 2015 was 24, against 32 for non-Muslims. Switching, meanwhile, is projected to cost Christianity some 72 million adherents over the same period, with no net loss to Islam.8
