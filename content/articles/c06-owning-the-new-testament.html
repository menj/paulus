If so much of Paul's teaching came from sources other than revelation, how did his writings come to dominate the New Testament over the gospels that record the direct teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him)? Why are most of the books of the New Testament letters of Paul and so few the records of the disciples?

This count applies the second rule of [paulus_link slug="the-measure" anchor="s-the-unity-of-truth"]the measure[/paulus_link], the denial of ultimate contradictions, to the book Paul came to own.

The scholarly mainstream measures his share in the same terms. About half of the New Testament, E. P. Sanders writes, "stems from Paul and the people whom he influenced", and Paul is "often considered to be the most important person after Jesus in the history of Christianity".1

The language of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam

Any assessment of Paul's dominance in the New Testament has to reckon with the language and culture of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam himself. Historical and linguistic study shows that the main language he used in his life and preaching was Aramaic, the language of Palestine at the time. The Gospel of Mark preserves several of his phrases in Aramaic even though the whole text is in Greek: "Talitha cumi,"2 "Ephphatha"3 and "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?"4

That shows the original Injīl was very probably delivered in Aramaic, and what we have inherited in Greek is a rendering or a secondary narrative. Some scholars hold that the Gospel of Matthew once existed in Aramaic before it was lost to time. The dominance of Greek in the New Testament text should therefore be read as a reframing of the original revelation in a foreign cultural and linguistic setting.5

The gospels that were set aside

[paulus_figure name="nag-hammadi"]Nag Hammadi Codex II, fourth century, Coptic Museum, Cairo, with the Gospel of Thomas and other texts the churches that followed Paul did not keep.[/paulus_figure]

In the time of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam he had many followers who wrote down his sermons and teachings after his departure. Foremost among them were the twelve apostles named in the New Testament. From the records of these followers come the writings collected under the name "gospel," each named for its writer: the Gospel of Marcion, the Gospel of the Egyptians, the Gospel of Eve, the Gospel of Judas, the Gospel of Nicodemus, the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Matthias, the Gospel of the Twelve, the Gospel of Truth, the Gospel of Mary, the Gospel of Andrew, the Gospel of Philip, and others.6

These gospels are said to contain the teaching of Satan and to contradict the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. In truth they are the gospels closest to the original Injīl, never yet infiltrated by the teachings of Paul.

Other gospels belonged to the Jewish followers of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam who kept his law, among them a gospel of the Ebionites that Michael Goulder dates to about 150. Their writings were lost with the communities that kept them, and survive only in quotations by the church fathers who wrote against them.7

Luke, traditionally identified as the author of the Gospel of Luke and of Acts, was Paul's personal physician.8 In writing his "gospel" he brought in Paul's teachings of his own accord.

The proof is in Acts, also written by the author of Luke, where the inclination to exalt Paul is unmistakable.9 Luke all but makes a cult of Paul in his writing.

The selection tells its own story. The New Testament gives the impression of a single, developing body of belief because it is a selection of writings, and Michael Goulder names the selectors: "naturally it was selected by the winning mission, that is the Paulines." Hence its contents, in his account: the letters of Paul and his followers, and four Gospels, "two of them ultra-Pauline and two building bridges to Jerusalem."10

Paul wrote first

In the chronology of composition, Paul's letters are among the earliest written documents in the New Testament. Letters such as 1 Thessalonians and Galatians are believed to have been written in the 50s CE, a decade or two after the departure of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. The Synoptic Gospels (Mark, Matthew and Luke) were written later, between about 65 and 90 CE, and the Gospel of John around 95 to 100 CE.

Historically, then, Paul's teaching came before the written gospels and very probably shaped the theology they contain.11 Scribes wrote the earliest complete copy of the New Testament, Codex Sinaiticus, in the middle of the fourth century, some three hundred years after ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.12

So it is no surprise that of the 27 books of the New Testament, 14 are said to be Paul's.13 Only seven of them are widely accepted by scholars as authentic writings of Paul.14 The other letters in the New Testament are the work of Paul's followers and supporters.15

Two Pauls: the man and the canon

The count divides the case in two. Six letters carry Paul's name and are doubted or rejected as his: 2 Thessalonians, Colossians and Ephesians, and the three Pastoral letters to Timothy and Titus, which most scholars assign to his school after his death. The letter to the Hebrews names no author at all, and the church credited it to him only later.16

Acts is a later and weaker witness still. Its author does not give his name, and the book has been dated anywhere from 80 to 150 CE, three decades or more after Paul's letters.17

This site keeps the two apart. Where a charge concerns Paul himself, what he taught and did, it rests on the seven letters that scholars accept as his, with Acts as a later and weaker witness.18

Where a charge concerns the religion that bears his name, all thirteen letters count, because the church received every one of them as Paul's, read them as scripture and built its doctrine on them. A letter written in his name is marked as such wherever it is quoted.

The line bears on the doctrine of scripture itself. The verse most often cited for the inspiration of the whole Bible, "All scripture is given by inspiration of God," stands in 2 Timothy, one of the letters written in his name.19 The verse before it names the scripture it means: the holy scriptures that Timothy had known "from a child," which were the Jewish scriptures, since no Gospel yet existed.20

The early church itself shows why the line matters. In the second century two opposed movements each claimed Paul: the Pastoral letters made him the patron of the ordered household, marriage and obedient wives, and the Acts of Paul and Thecla made him the preacher of virginity who drew women out of their homes.

Each wrote its Paul in his name.21 The Paul of the canon is the Paul the church chose, and the religion of Paul is built on that choice.
