Paul often presents himself as an apostle on the strength of personal experience and the direct revelation he claims to have received. He states that the gospel he brought came from no man but was received through the revelation of Jesus Christ.1 The statement raises a serious question.

If the revelation was real, why do the disciples who received the teaching of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam (ʿalayhi al-salām, peace be upon him) directly never refer to Paul? Disciples such as Peter and Barnabas often disputed Paul's approach as contrary to the law. The tension between Paul and Peter over obedience to the Torah reflects a basic difference in their apostolic method.

Jesus often warned that no one should set himself up as absolute spiritual leader, and insisted that all his followers are equal as brethren.2 Paul declared himself a preacher, an apostle and a teacher, a statement that reflects his effort to build personal authority.3

When we examine the content of Paul's letters, we find claims of apparitions, mystical encounters and unseen experiences that form the basis of his "apostleship". These narratives are full of contradiction and vagueness, and they lack any witness from a reliable external source. Some appear only in Paul's own letters, without confirmation in the gospels or in Acts.

The five hundred witnesses

Among the most suspect is his note in 1 Corinthians that the risen Jesus supposedly appeared to "more than five hundred at once":

6ἔπειτα ὤφθη ἐπάνω πεντακοσίοις ἀδελφοῖς ἐφάπαξ, ἐξ ὧν οἱ πλείονες μένουσιν ἕως ἄρτι, τινὲς δὲ ἐκοιμήθησαν·

6After that, he was seen of above five hundred brethren at once; of whom the greater part remain unto this present, but some are fallen asleep.4

Prima facie, a claim of five hundred witnesses shifts the burden to the doubter; on inspection it names none. The statement finds no support in any canonical gospel. Mark, Matthew, Luke and John are silent about this mass appearance. So is Acts, which should have recorded so important an event if it happened; instead it plainly mentions only about 120 disciples gathered after the crucifixion.5

More doubtful still, Paul names none of the "500 witnesses." By adding that "some are fallen asleep," he seems to head off any further inquiry into the testimony.

Stranger yet, none of the other early followers of al-Masīḥ, Peter, James or any other disciple, ever refers to the event, in any recorded writing or speech. The absence of any repetition or support for the claim in any early Christian source suggests that Paul may have invented the story to strengthen the argument for the resurrection at the center of his new teaching.

The claim of an appearance to 500 is the plainest example of how the post-crucifixion narrative in Paul's writings rests on rhetorical invention in support of his theological agenda.6 It reflects Paul's tendency to add mystical and dramatic elements with no empirical basis, the modus operandi of the "personal revelations" he so often claimed.

Satan as an angel of light

Beyond the claim of a crowd of unnamed witnesses, Paul also presents a personal unseen experience as the chief basis of his "apostleship". As discussed earlier, Paul, then known as Saul, set out for Damascus to hunt more followers of Jesus Christ.

On the way he claimed to have "met" ʿĪsā ibn Maryam through an experience from the sky in the form of a "bright shining light." After it he is said to have declared his repentance and professed himself a chosen follower of ʿĪsā ibn Maryam.7

[paulus_figure name="poussin-ecstasy"]Nicolas Poussin, The Ecstasy of Saint Paul, about 1650, Louvre, Paris. Paul borne up to the third heaven (2 Corinthians 12:2), the kind of private vision on which his authority rested.[/paulus_figure]

As explained earlier, the story exists in three different versions. All three share one element: the claim that he met ʿĪsā ibn Maryam in a mysterious event involving "a bright light from heaven."

3ἐν δὲ τῷ πορεύεσθαι ἐγένετο αὐτὸν ἐγγίζειν τῇ Δαμασκῷ, ἐξαίφνης τε αὐτὸν περιήστραψεν φῶς ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ, 4καὶ πεσὼν ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν ἤκουσεν φωνὴν λέγουσαν αὐτῷ Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις;

3And as he journeyed, he came near Damascus: and suddenly there shined round about him a light from heaven: 4And he fell to the earth, and heard a voice saying unto him, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me?8

6Ἐγένετο δέ μοι πορευομένῳ καὶ ἐγγίζοντι τῇ Δαμασκῷ περὶ μεσημβρίαν ἐξαίφνης ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ περιαστράψαι φῶς ἱκανὸν περὶ ἐμέ,

6And it came to pass, that, as I made my journey, and was come nigh unto Damascus about noon, suddenly there shone from heaven a great light round about me.9

12Ἐν οἷς πορευόμενος εἰς τὴν Δαμασκὸν μετʼ ἐξουσίας καὶ ἐπιτροπῆς τῆς τῶν ἀρχιερέων 13ἡμέρας μέσης κατὰ τὴν ὁδὸν εἶδον, βασιλεῦ, οὐρανόθεν ὑπὲρ τὴν λαμπρότητα τοῦ ἡλίου περιλάμψαν με φῶς καὶ τοὺς σὺν ἐμοὶ πορευομένους· 14πάντων τε καταπεσόντων ἡμῶν εἰς τὴν γῆν ἤκουσα φωνὴν λέγουσαν πρός με τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ· Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις; σκληρόν σοι πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν.

12Whereupon as I went to Damascus with authority and commission from the chief priests, 13At midday, O king, I saw in the way a light from heaven, above the brightness of the sun, shining round about me and them which journeyed with me. 14And when we were all fallen to the earth, I heard a voice speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue, Saul, Saul, why persecutest thou me? it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks.10

Paul claims that a "bright light" from heaven surrounded him at his mysterious "meeting" with the figure said to be ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. Even if the event happened, it does not follow that Paul met ʿĪsā ibn Maryam. The New Testament itself states that the Devil can disguise himself as an angel of light:

14καὶ οὐ θαῦμα, αὐτὸς γὰρ ὁ Σατανᾶς μετασχηματίζεται εἰς ἄγγελον φωτός·

14And no marvel; for Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light.11

Is the resemblance between the appearance of the Devil, as Paul himself wrote it in the New Testament, and the event he experienced in Acts not plain?
