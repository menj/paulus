Paul died as a state criminal, sentenced by the imperial power regarded as the enemy of the movement he supposedly represented. Paul's execution by the Roman regime, the same regime that according to church doctrine also killed Jesus, raises serious questions about the legitimacy of his "apostleship". His death is recorded in no book of the New Testament, including Acts, which consistently documents Paul's travels and apostolic activity and then breaks off abruptly while he is still under house arrest in Rome.1

More puzzling still, the absence of any narrative of revelation or spiritual witness about the death of Paul, so dominant a figure in the building of Christian doctrine, is an extraordinary silence. If he was the "apostle to the Gentiles," why is there no record of his end in the sacred text?

Why does the story of his death exist only in secondary church writings that appeared decades later,2 without witness or revelation? The death of Paul as a state criminal, passed over in silence by the canonical texts, raises a serious theological doubt: is this the end of a messenger of God, or of a religious figure shaped by politics, confirmed by the church, and abandoned by revelation?

Although no book of the New Testament, Acts included, records the last moments of Paul's life, various secondary church writings built a narrative of his death. In 1 Clement, the letter written by Clement of Rome around 90 CE, there is this note on Paul:

By reason of jealousy and strife Paul by his example pointed out the prize of patient endurance. After that he had been seven times in bonds, had been driven into exile, had been stoned, had preached in the East and in the West, he won the noble renown which was the reward of his faith, having taught righteousness unto the whole world and having reached the farthest bounds of the West; and when he had borne his testimony before the rulers, so he departed from the world and went unto the holy place, having been found a notable pattern of patient endurance.3

The text leaves unstated that Paul died in Rome, and unstated that he died a martyr. That interpretation was first given by modern scholars such as Raymond Brown, on the strength of vague phrases like "the farthest bounds of the West" and "went unto the holy place."4

Three centuries later Eusebius of Caesarea wrote in his Ecclesiastical History that they, Peter and Paul, "suffered martyrdom at that time … Paul was beheaded in Rome, and Peter crucified."5 The tradition was later reinforced through church liturgy, above all the Feast of Saints Peter and Paul on 29 June. The traditional site of Paul's execution is supposedly the church of San Paolo alle Tre Fontane, and the great basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls was built over what is said to be the place of his burial.

The scene rests on the secondary writings discussed above; no book of the New Testament records it.[/paulus_figure] [paulus_figure name="tomb-of-paul"]The sarcophagus beneath the high altar of the Basilica of Saint Paul Outside the Walls, Rome, venerated as Paul's tomb.[/paulus_figure]

In 2009 Pope Benedict XVI announced that bone fragments drawn from this sarcophagus had been carbon-dated to the first or second century, and said the result "seems to confirm" the tradition that they are Paul's.6 The scientist in charge of the tests, Ulderico Santamaria, director of a diagnostic laboratory for the Vatican Museums, would say no more than that the result "doesn't make certain, but also doesn't exclude" that the remains are Paul's.7 The Vatican's own laboratory would not put Paul's name to the bones.

Later versions circulating in Greek, Latin and Syriac give a picture more symbolic than historical. In one version, when Paul was beheaded milk spurted from his neck in place of blood, a symbol of purity that rests on no historical fact and was shaped to stress his holy status and spiritual confirmation.8 The narrative also mentions a woman named Lucina who took Paul's body and buried it on her land on the Via Ostiensis, laying the basis for the liturgical rites and the veneration of a particular place in Rome as a site of pilgrimage.9

Allegorical elements of this kind show how Paul's death was reinterpreted through the theological frame of the church . They reflect an effort to shape an ex post sanctity, setting Paul's death within a sacred mythology aligned with the ideological needs of the church.

All this information derives from no revelation and exists in no canonical book. It rests only on oral tradition, on apocryphal texts such as the Acts of Paul and the Acts of Peter, and on free interpretation of 2 Timothy 4:13, which says only that Paul left a cloak at Troas, with no indication of death.10

More puzzling still, the Catholic Church claims that Paul's skull is now in the Lateran Basilica, beside the skull of Peter. In 665 CE Pope Vitalian is said to have sent several relics of Paul to Britain, to King Oswy.11 All this only adds to the obscurity of one important question: if Paul was the great apostle the church claims, why is the story of his death nowhere mentioned in the sacred texts?

Eutychus

Before everything ends, Acts leaves us with one moment hard to forget, for its tedium:

9καθεζόμενος δέ τις νεανίας ὀνόματι Εὔτυχος ἐπὶ τῆς θυρίδος, καταφερόμενος ὕπνῳ βαθεῖ διαλεγομένου τοῦ Παύλου ἐπὶ πλεῖον, κατενεχθεὶς ἀπὸ τοῦ ὕπνου ἔπεσεν ἀπὸ τοῦ τριστέγου κάτω καὶ ἤρθη νεκρός.

9And there sat in a window a certain young man named Eutychus, being fallen into a deep sleep: and as Paul was long preaching, he sunk down with sleep, and fell down from the third loft, and was taken up dead.12

Is this the face of an "apostle" at the end of the age? In the middle of preaching supposedly sourced in revelation, Paul's sermon was long and dull enough to kill a listener. No prophecy, no charisma, no resurrection.

Only a young listener too weary to go on living. If that is no anticlimax, what is?

The argument that Paul's death proves the truth of his message is a non sequitur. Many figures in history, founders of heresies, political figures, even cult leaders, have died for their own beliefs.

Dying for a conviction shows the depth of commitment, and nothing more. With no confirmed revelation, no witness of the community and no recognition in the sacred text, Paul's death, however sincere in intention, cannot raise him to the rank of apostle.

What Paul went through left its mark on his personality, and it also shaped the pattern of the teaching he brought, a teaching born of the rationalization of his own inner crisis, and never of the continuity of revelation. The emotional conflict evident in his letters, above all the swing between remorse and self-justification, suggests that the dogma he introduced was more than a theological system.

It was the reflection of a soul struggling with guilt and the need to justify its past. Small wonder that the frame of faith he built is full of paradox and inner tension, from the question of the Torah to the concept of redemption, all centered on a narrative that tries to heal, or at least to cover, a psychological wound that never healed.
